1. Abstract

Ferminux is an independent layer-1 blockchain with an EVM-compatible execution layer. It has its own native coin, FMX, with a hard cap of 100,000,000 and a fully specified emission schedule; its own consensus; and a first-party set of applications — a two-way bridge to BNB Smart Chain, a decentralised exchange, a fully-collateralised dollar stablecoin, a web wallet, a block explorer and a token launchpad — all of which are live at the time of writing.

The network launched on 20 August 2026 as an Ethash proof-of-work chain. Within its first two days it demonstrated the structural weakness of small proof-of-work chains: rented hashrate raised the difficulty, left, and the chain took nine hours to recover. The operator's response — documented in §3 — was a scheduled move to authority consensus, operated by a set of foundation signers on several hosting providers, with the limits of that choice stated plainly rather than dressed up. This paper is written in that spirit: what exists, how it works, what it promises, and what it does not.

3. Consensus

3.1 What it is

Ferminux uses authority consensus. A set of authorised signers confirms blocks in rotation every 7 seconds; there were five at the fork and four on 26 September 2026. They are not bonded: no stake backs them and there is nothing to slash, so the only thing standing behind a signer is the foundation's operation of it. The signer set is operated by the foundation. This is not trustless proof of stake and we do not claim it is. FMX staking, when it launches, is a yield programme funded from the ecosystem allocation; it does not secure the chain.

Concretely: block n is confirmed in turn by signer n mod N, where N is the size of the set, with a fallback so that a single unavailable signer does not stop production. An empty block is confirmed whether or not anyone is transacting. Production continues as long as more than half of the signer processes are running.

The authority engine is a Clique-based wrapper compiled into the node software and activates at a fixed block, 160,000. Below that block the chain is proof-of-work exactly as launched; from that block onward the authorised signers confirm blocks. The fork block, the signer set and the reward-sink contract are compiled constants — there is no configuration file that can change them on a running network, which is deliberate: every node that runs the release is on the same rules.

3.2 Why it changed

On 21 August 2026 the chain stopped producing blocks for four hours and thirty-six minutes, and did not recover for nine: blocks 20,570 to 20,577 took nine hours between them, crawling at roughly a tenth of the hashrate the difficulty had been set for. Rented GPU hashrate had set that difficulty, and Ethash only lowers it when a block is found — so each block bought at most a 4.83% reduction, and the chain ratcheted back rather than snapping back. Nothing downstream could work: the bridge could not confirm transfers and the explorer froze.

That failure is structural to a small proof-of-work chain: whoever brings the most hashrate sets a difficulty that only they can sustain, and when they leave the chain halts. Authority consensus removes the dependency on hashrate entirely. It does so at a cost, stated in §3.4.

All of them are operated by one party. They run on separate machines at four unrelated hosting providers, one per provider, so no single provider outage stops block production — but they answer to the same operator. Each signer is a dedicated process with no remote control interface; its key is unlocked only on its own machine. The signer set can change two ways, and the second one matters more than the first. Normally it takes an in-band vote by a majority of current signers, recorded in block headers and visible to anyone. But a break-glass path also exists: two of three foundation-held owner keys can replace the signer set outright, with no signer vote at all. It is there so the chain can be recovered if signer keys are lost, and every use of it is a block anyone can see — but it means the honest statement is that the same party which runs every signer can also replace the whole set at will.

After the fork, each block's reward is split three ways: 40% to the sealing signer, 50% to the FMXRewardSink contract (0x691E5275BF346FfFa0B30174dDBeDfCC078dd8D6), and 10% to the treasury. The sink is the funding source for ecosystem programmes, including the staking yield programme in §7.

3.4 What we are not claiming

  • It is not decentralised. Several signers, one operator. Proof-of-work, whatever its faults, let anyone with hardware participate in producing blocks. This does not.
  • History is secured by keys, not by work. Under proof-of-work, rewriting the chain cost real electricity. Under authority consensus a majority of signers could rewrite it at no cost. The bridge is defended against this with signed checkpoints and a reorg-depth limit of 64 blocks compiled into every node, not by the consensus rule itself.
  • Losing half the signers halts the chain. If half or more of the signer keys or machines are lost at once (two of the four on 26 September 2026), block production stops until the foundation restores them from escrow. This is a known, rehearsed recovery, and it is still a halt.
  • Staking does not secure anything. The staking programme pays FMX from a funded pool to holders who lock FMX. It votes on nothing and can neither halt nor split the chain.

4. FMX

4.1 Supply

Stated hard cap 100,000,000 FMX. To be exact about what that is: no code checks it — there is no supply counter in the node that would refuse a block for exceeding it. What the software enforces is the emission schedule, and that schedule converges to about 32.5 million (§4.2), so the cap is a ceiling the chain cannot reach rather than a rule it is stopped by. 4.2 Emission

The network launched at 6 FMX per block. From block 20,000 the base reward is 1 FMX per block, halving every 4,500,000 blocks (roughly annually at 7-second blocks). After the authority fork at block 160,000 the per-block reward is 0.25 FMX, split 40 / 50 / 10 as described in §3.3. On this schedule total supply converges to about 32.5 million FMX: the 30 million premine plus 2.47 million ever issued as block rewards (0.12 M at the launch rate, 0.14 M before the authority fork, and 2.21 M across the whole quartered tail). The 100 million cap is therefore a ceiling the chain never approaches, not a target — roughly 67.5 million FMX will never exist. The schedule is enforced by the node software and visible in every block's reward.

4.3 Foundation lock-up

To align the operator with holders for the first year of authority consensus, FMX is locked in an immutable contract, FoundationLock at 0xC0E01D9F49eE0967F34e1CB045B74D3Aefac189d, which cannot release funds before 21 August 2027 and whose sole beneficiary is the foundation multisig. Its balance and unlock time are readable on-chain at any time (locked(), unlockAt()) and are displayed live on the security page. The lock is being funded in tranches; 150,292 FMX were locked at the time of writing, against a stated target of 1,000,000.

4.4 Price

No price is published anywhere by the foundation. FMX's market is the Ferminux DEX on chain 3961, where the WFMX/AZNT pool (0xbab12e7B817F0686e11949eC06697235DC146845, its liquidity locked until 20 August 2027) has traded since 20 August 2026; the price is whatever that pool says. Bridged FMX (wFMX) also trades in a small wFMX/BNB pool on PancakeSwap, funded by the foundation.

6. Security model

6.1 Keys and custody

  • Protocol-level authority on each chain is a 2-of-3 multisig; each chain has its own (the Ferminux multisig for Ferminux contracts, 0x15D0791d49A089863243BE2C2050e5d26E1bBA9c for BSC). Bridge parameter changes that enlarge what the bridge can do pass through a 48-hour timelock. Three do not, deliberately: pause and pauseToken (a hot pauser key, so a suspected incident can be stopped in one transaction) and decreaseTokenLimits (the owner may always tighten a cap immediately). The rule is that anything which increases risk waits 48 hours and anything which reduces it takes effect at once — not that every change is timelocked.
  • Bridge validator keys live on three separate hosts and nowhere else. Signer keys live one per host, escrowed offline, on a recovery procedure that has been rehearsed end-to-end under 30 minutes.
  • Break-glass: if the signer set is lost, a 2-of-3 owner signature over the chain ID, block number, parent hash and new signer list reinstates production. The procedure is scripted and was live-tested.

6.2 Chain rules that limit damage

  • A 64-block reorg cap compiled into every node: no node will reorganise deeper than 64 blocks under an authority head, whatever it is offered.
  • Fork choice prefers an authority head over any proof-of-work head after the fork block, so a node still producing proof-of-work blocks on old software cannot carry the network.
  • The bridge waits for finality it can verify — confirmations and, after the fork, a checkpoint published to BSC — and fails closed when it cannot.

6.3 Operations

  • Every signer and witness node runs as an unprivileged user under a systemd sandbox; signer processes expose no HTTP or WebSocket interface at all.
  • A chain watchdog on three hosts raises an alert on a stall, and after the fork on a dead signer, a falling in-turn rate, a skewed signer clock or a halt.
  • Every change to consensus-affecting software is rehearsed on an isolated copy of the live chain before it reaches production; the rehearsal logs are kept with the release.

8. Verify it yourself

  • Genesis hash from any node: ferminux-geth dumpgenesis → 0x1b62e052ee210c433440b9cd21b93b3e6cdc813fe63674c842bca3967d92fadf.
  • The fork block and signer set are printed in the node's start-up banner and are readable after the fork via clique_getSigners and clique_getSnapshot on any node.
  • The signer of any block is recoverable from the block header's signature.
  • Bridge collateral and lock-up balances are read live from the contracts on the security page at ferminux.net/security.html.
  • Release binaries ship with SHA-256 checksums; the installer refuses a binary that does not match.

9. Disclosures

This document describes software and contracts as deployed on 22 August 2026 and will be revised as they change. It is not an offer to sell, a solicitation, or investment advice. FMX is a utility coin that pays for transactions on the Ferminux network; it has no guaranteed value and no price until a market exists. The authority consensus described in §3 is operated by a single party; holders should weigh that before relying on the chain for anything of value. The staking programme, when it launches, pays from a finite pool and makes no promise beyond its published balance and depletion date. Nothing here should be read as a claim of decentralisation, of proof of stake, or of regulatory status in any jurisdiction.
